pH and Acidity

pH is the negative base-10 logarithm of the hydrogen-ion concentration, pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Because the scale is logarithmic, a drop of one pH unit represents a tenfold increase in [H⁺]. Pure water is neutral at pH 7; values below 7 are acidic. Most freshwater aquatic life is adapted to a narrow circum-neutral band (≈6.0–8.0).

Acid Rain

Combustion of fossil fuels releases sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). In the atmosphere these form sulfuric and nitric acids: SO₂ + ½O₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ and 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃. These strong acids dissociate fully, lowering the pH of precipitation and the water bodies it feeds.

Corrosion and the Reactivity Series

Corrosion is the oxidation of a metal by its environment. In acidic water the high concentration of H⁺ ions accelerates the reaction M + nH⁺ → Mⁿ⁺ + (n/2)H₂. The reactivity series ranks metals by how readily they lose electrons. More reactive metals (Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Cu) corrode faster, so in this simulation each metal's corrosion rate equals its reactivity score multiplied by an acidity factor that rises sharply as pH falls.

Metal-Ion Pollution

As metals corrode they release ions (e.g. Fe²⁺, Zn²⁺, Al³⁺, Cu²⁺) into the water column. Many of these are toxic to aquatic organisms even at low concentrations, disrupting gill function and enzyme activity. Rising dissolved-metal loads lower the Water Quality Index.

Biodiversity Impacts

Acidification and metal pollution act as abiotic stressors. Sensitive species (mayflies, salmonid eggs, molluscs) disappear first, simplifying the food web and reducing biodiversity. Below about pH 5 reproductive failure becomes widespread; below pH 4 most vertebrate life cannot survive. Maintaining buffering capacity is therefore essential to ecosystem resilience.
